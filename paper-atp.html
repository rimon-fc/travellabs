ATP: Consent and recovery for agentic travel

A shared account of what was approved, what was booked and what still needs attention.

ABSTRACT

A trip can span several businesses, each with different rules for holds, confirmation, payment and cancellation. The Agentic Travel Protocol (ATP) proposes a common way to describe those actions and the permission behind them. This paper examines the draft v0.3 implementation supplied with keel. It connects scoped traveller consent, declared capabilities, trip-level coordination and Agentic Payment for Travel using a virtual credit card (VCC). It also studies partial failure: confirmed parts may need to be cancelled, and cancellation may itself fail. We argue that a useful protocol must preserve unresolved obligations as clearly as successful bookings. The code provides concrete mechanisms and failure-test definitions, but this review establishes neither universal interoperability nor a guarantee that every outside action can be reversed.

THE CENTRAL IDEAA failed trip is not finished until each booking and payment has a known outcome—or a visible owner for what remains unresolved.

1. Travel has more than one moment of commitment

Searching does not reserve a seat. A hold may expire. A confirmation may create a cancellation obligation. A payment may succeed while the reply is lost. An agent needs these distinctions before it can act responsibly on someone’s behalf.

The earlier ATF report identifies a travel-specific extension to agentic commerce as an opportunity. The supplied keel snapshot contains a concrete ATP extension, explicitly labelled alpha and tied to a draft protocol version. This paper studies that implementation as a basis for discussion, not as an adopted global standard.

IN ONE SENTENCEATP gives the agent, the traveller and the connected business a shared vocabulary for permission and commitment.

2. Permission must survive the journey

ATP’s traveller consent model carries limited authority. The documented checks cover a valid signature, an expiry, the permitted operation and a maximum trip value. Commitment uses a single-use consent gate. Permission to search does not automatically become permission to hold, commit or cancel.

Those limits answer “may this agent act?” They do not, by themselves, prove that a changed room or itinerary still matches what the traveller approved. Offer details and terms must remain tied to the decision. Material changes require the application to recheck the purchase and, when necessary, ask again.

Capability declarations matter just as much. A business may support searching but not holds or automatic confirmation. The snapshot distinguishes a coordinator’s own reservation record from a source-confirmed reservation. A record inside the agent’s system must never be presented as proof that outside inventory is secured.

4. Case study: two parts confirm, the third fails

ILLUSTRATIVE CASE

The trip fails. Some of its obligations remain.

Imagine a flight, a stay and an activity. The first two confirm; the activity cannot. The coordinator begins recovery by cancelling the confirmed parts in reverse order. If both cancellations succeed, the record can show what was released and what payment follow-up is required.

Now let the stay cancellation fail as well. The reviewed code continues recovering the other confirmed part and records the failed cancellation on the stay. It can mark the trip transaction cancelled while that leg remains unresolved. A customer-facing summary must surface that remaining obligation. “Trip cancelled” alone is insufficient.

This recovery pattern is often called a saga: a sequence of actions with follow-up actions intended to undo their effects where possible. It is not a single indivisible transaction across every connected business. The supplied failure tests specifically include cancellation failures.

The record should answer four questions: Which parts actually confirmed? Which were successfully released? What happened to payment? Who is responsible for the remaining issue? That is the operational meaning of a clear outcome.

5. Agentic Payment for Travel

The payment layer records instructions alongside reservations. A virtual credit card (VCC) can constrain the payment’s amount, validity and use. The documented payment record links booking and card references so the outcome can be reconciled. A VCC is a controlled payment instrument; it does not establish whether the room, dates or cancellation terms are correct.

Payment and reservation state must be followed together. A confirmed booking does not prove settlement, and an attempted payment is not proof that funds moved. The reviewed implementation distinguishes payment states and uses guarded responses when a configured payment connection is unavailable. Completing a real transaction still requires working providers and their actual outcomes.

After booking, the same discipline applies to changes and price-drop rebooking. Secure and verify the replacement before releasing the original where the product and cancellation terms permit it. A price lock requires explicit coverage and a party responsible for it; a timer or saved quote is not sufficient proof of protection. These are requirements for products built on the protocol, not universal guarantees made by this alpha.

7. A protocol needs failure trials

For each trip with several parts, inject a failure at every confirmation position. Then inject failures into recovery itself. Delay replies, repeat requests, expire holds and change terms after approval. Include cases where a remote action succeeds but its acknowledgement is lost.

Report unresolved obligations, duplicate external actions, time to a known outcome and cases needing human intervention. Count a case as resolved only when the individual booking and payment outcomes justify that conclusion. A terminal status inside the coordinator is not enough.

The protocol also needs independent implementations to test the meaning of each capability and state. Interoperability cannot be established by having one implementation agree with itself.

8. From an alpha to a shared standard

The opportunity is to define the missing travel-specific open standard: one account of consent, capabilities, booking state and payment outcomes that agents and travel businesses can share. That could position FCTG to help shape how AI agents book and transact travel globally. It remains an ambition requiring wider participation, governance and evidence.

Open questions include expired authority during a long recovery, inconsistent provider replies, disputed payments and liability for unresolved actions. A standard should make those conditions explicit. It should not hide them behind a promise that every trip always succeeds or is fully undone.
